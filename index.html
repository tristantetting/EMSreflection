Post-Call Reflection Log.

⚠
De-identify everything. No names, addresses, facility names, run numbers, exact dates of unusual calls, or details specific enough to recognize the patient. Write "elderly male, respiratory distress" — not who, not where. This log is for your reflection only, kept separate from any run report.
Log entry

Shorthand title & date

A phrase you'll recognize in two years. "Cardiac arrest, family in the room." "Refusal that bothered me."

Why this matters: admissions committees notice breadth of exposure. These tags let you see your range — and gaps — at a glance.
Scene

The clinical picture

What were you dispatched to, what did you actually find, and how did it evolve? Concrete and de-identified: a vital trend, a presentation, the moment your assessment changed.

Why this matters: specific clinical detail is what makes an AMCAS anecdote feel real instead of generic. "I responded to emergencies" is forgettable; a scene is not.
Intervention

What I did — my actual role

Your hands, your decisions, your communication. Where were you in the flow of the call — leading, assisting, watching, talking to family?

Why this matters: committees probe whether your hours involved real responsibility. Documenting your specific role now makes that easy to demonstrate later.
Patch

The human moment

Something said, a silence, a decision point, a place where communication mattered as much as the protocol. Dialogue if you remember it.

Why this matters: this is the material personal statements, most-meaningful essays, and MMI answers are actually built from. Not the skill — the person.
Debrief

What I felt — honestly

Surprised, calm, out of my depth, frustrated, useless, proud. Unpolished is the point; nobody reads this but you.

Why this matters: secondaries constantly ask "describe a time your perspective changed" or "a challenging interaction." Honest recorded emotion is what you can't reconstruct from memory later.
Review

Uncertainty & what I'd do differently

Where you weren't sure. What you'd change — clinically or interpersonally. Growth-mindset material lives here.

Why this matters: interviewers respect reflection on imperfection far more than polished success stories. This is your bank of "tell me about a mistake" answers.
Signal

One line: what this taught me about being a physician

One sentence only. If you can't say it in one line, the lesson isn't distilled yet — and that's fine, leave it blank and come back.

Competencies

Which AAMC core competencies did this call touch?

Tag honestly, not aspirationally. When a secondary asks about teamwork or ethics, you'll filter the logbook by these tags and have real stories waiting.
